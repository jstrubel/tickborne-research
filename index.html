Understanding Tick-Borne Infections

A comprehensive guide to vector-borne diseases — what they are, how you get them, and what to do about it.

On This Page

What Are Tick-Borne Diseases?

Tick-borne diseases (TBDs) are infections transmitted to humans and animals through the bite of infected ticks. These tiny arachnids can carry a variety of bacteria, parasites, and viruses that cause illness ranging from mild to severe.

The term "vector-borne" refers to any disease transmitted by a living organism (the vector) — in this case, ticks. While mosquitoes transmit malaria and Zika, ticks are responsible for Lyme disease, Bartonella, Babesiosis, and many others.

Why This Matters

Tick-borne infections are among the fastest-growing infectious disease categories in the United States and Europe. The CDC estimates over 476,000 Americans are diagnosed with Lyme disease annually, and many experts believe the true number is significantly higher due to underreporting and diagnostic challenges.

How You Get Infected

Infection occurs when an infected tick attaches to your skin and feeds on your blood. During feeding, pathogens in the tick's saliva or gut can transfer into your bloodstream.

Tick Attachment Time

The risk of transmission increases the longer a tick remains attached. For Lyme disease, the traditional guideline suggested 36-48 hours, but recent research shows transmission can occur much faster — sometimes within hours.

Not Just Ticks

While ticks are the primary vector, some pathogens (like Bartonella) can also be transmitted through flea bites, cat scratches, and potentially other biting insects. Blood transfusions can transmit Babesia.

Common Tick Species

Black-legged Tick
Ixodes scapularis

Also called the "deer tick." Primary vector for Lyme disease, Anaplasmosis, Babesiosis, and Powassan virus in the Eastern and Midwestern US.

Western Black-legged Tick
Ixodes pacificus

Found along the Pacific coast. Transmits Lyme disease and Anaplasmosis in Western states.

Lone Star Tick
Amblyomma americanum

Aggressive biter found in the Southeast. Transmits Ehrlichiosis and can trigger Alpha-gal syndrome (red meat allergy).

American Dog Tick
Dermacentor variabilis

Widespread across the US. Primary vector for Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever and Tularemia.

The Pathogens

Ticks can carry multiple pathogens simultaneously, which is why co-infections are common and can complicate both diagnosis and treatment.

Bacteria

Lyme Disease

Borrelia burgdorferi (US), B. garinii, B. afzelii (Europe)

The most common tick-borne illness in North America and Europe. Caused by spiral-shaped bacteria (spirochetes) that can disseminate throughout the body, affecting joints, the nervous system, and heart.

Key symptoms: Erythema migrans (bull's-eye rash) in ~70% of cases, fatigue, joint pain, neurological symptoms, brain fog
Standard treatment: Doxycycline, amoxicillin, or cefuroxime for 2-4 weeks (acute); longer courses may be needed for disseminated or chronic cases
Bacteria

Bartonella

B. henselae, B. quintana, B. bacilliformis, and others

Often called "cat scratch disease" but increasingly recognized as a significant tick-borne pathogen. Known for its neuropsychiatric manifestations and ability to infect blood vessel linings (endothelial cells).

Key symptoms: Stretch mark-like skin striae, swollen lymph nodes, anxiety, rage/irritability, neurological issues, subcutaneous nodules
Standard treatment: Combination antibiotics often required — azithromycin, rifampin, doxycycline; fluoroquinolones for resistant cases
Parasite

Babesiosis

Babesia microti (US), B. duncani, B. divergens (Europe)

A malaria-like parasitic infection that invades and destroys red blood cells. Can range from asymptomatic to life-threatening, especially in immunocompromised individuals or those without a spleen.

Key symptoms: Night sweats, air hunger/sighing, fatigue, chills, hemolytic anemia, dark urine
Standard treatment: Atovaquone + azithromycin (mild-moderate); clindamycin + quinine (severe); exchange transfusion for critical cases

Other Tick-Borne Pathogens

Anaplasmosis

Anaplasma phagocytophilum

Infects white blood cells. Causes fever, headache, muscle pain. Treated with doxycycline.

Ehrlichiosis

Ehrlichia chaffeensis

Similar to Anaplasmosis. More common in southeastern US. Can be severe if untreated.

Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever

Rickettsia rickettsii

Potentially fatal if not treated early. Characteristic spotted rash. Requires immediate doxycycline.

Powassan Virus

Flavivirus

Rare but serious viral infection. Can cause encephalitis. No specific treatment; supportive care only.

Tick-Borne Relapsing Fever

Borrelia hermsii, B. miyamotoi

Related to Lyme but distinct. Causes recurring fevers. Treated with doxycycline or penicillin.

Tularemia

Francisella tularensis

Also known as "rabbit fever." Can be transmitted through tick bites, handling animals, or inhalation.

Common Symptoms

Tick-borne infections can affect virtually every organ system. Symptoms often overlap between different infections, making diagnosis challenging.

General / Systemic

  • Fatigue (often profound)
  • Fever, chills, sweats
  • Flu-like symptoms
  • Unexplained weight changes

Neurological

  • Brain fog, cognitive issues
  • Headaches, migraines
  • Numbness, tingling
  • Bell's palsy (facial drooping)
  • Memory problems

Psychiatric

  • Anxiety, panic attacks
  • Depression
  • Irritability, rage
  • Sleep disturbances
  • Depersonalization

Musculoskeletal

  • Joint pain (migratory)
  • Muscle aches
  • Neck stiffness
  • Swelling

Cardiovascular

  • Heart palpitations
  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath
  • POTS symptoms

Other

  • Light/sound sensitivity
  • Vision changes
  • Rashes (various types)
  • GI symptoms

Important Note

Many patients with tick-borne infections never recall a tick bite or develop the characteristic bull's-eye rash. If you have unexplained symptoms — especially a combination of fatigue, cognitive issues, and pain — and spend time outdoors, tick-borne infections should be considered.

Diagnosis Challenges

Diagnosing tick-borne infections is notoriously difficult. Standard testing has significant limitations, and many patients go years without answers.

Why Diagnosis Is Difficult

Test Sensitivity Issues

The standard two-tier Lyme test (ELISA + Western Blot) may miss up to 50% of cases, especially in early or late-stage disease. Tests measure antibody response, not the infection itself.

Timing Matters

Antibodies take 4-6 weeks to develop. Testing too early yields false negatives. But waiting means the infection can disseminate.

Co-Infections Complicate

Patients often have multiple tick-borne infections simultaneously. Each requires different testing, and one can mask or alter the presentation of another.

No Gold Standard

Unlike strep throat, there's no simple, definitive test. Diagnosis often requires clinical judgment plus specialty testing.

Testing Options

Standard Two-Tier Testing
ELISA screening followed by Western Blot confirmation. CDC-recommended but has sensitivity limitations.
Specialty Labs (IGeneX, Vibrant, etc.)
More sensitive testing panels that check for more antibody bands and additional species. Not universally accepted by mainstream medicine.
PCR Testing
Detects pathogen DNA directly. Highly specific but can miss infections if bacteria aren't circulating in blood.
Blood Smear (Babesia)
Microscopic examination can visualize Babesia parasites in red blood cells, though sensitivity is low.
Clinical Diagnosis
Lyme-literate physicians (LLMDs) often diagnose based on clinical presentation combined with test results, not test results alone.

Treatment Overview

Treatment approaches vary significantly depending on whether you're seeing a conventional infectious disease doctor or a Lyme-literate physician. Here's an overview of both perspectives.

Conventional Approach

  • 2-4 weeks of oral antibiotics for early Lyme
  • IV antibiotics for neurological involvement
  • Does not recognize chronic/persistent infection
  • Lingering symptoms attributed to "post-treatment Lyme disease syndrome" (PTLDS)
  • Limited co-infection testing

ILADS / Lyme-Literate Approach

  • Treatment duration guided by clinical response
  • Combination antibiotic protocols
  • Recognizes persistent infection as possibility
  • Aggressive co-infection testing and treatment
  • Addresses biofilms, immune dysfunction, detox
  • Integrative approaches (herbs, lifestyle, supplements)

Important Disclaimer

This information is educational and not medical advice. Treatment decisions should be made with a qualified healthcare provider who understands your individual situation. If you suspect you have a tick-borne infection, seek evaluation from a physician experienced in these conditions.
